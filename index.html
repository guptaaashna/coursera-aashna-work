Reflections on Technology and Education

The intersection of technology and education is rapidly evolving. We are moving away from traditional rote learning towards more interactive, personalized, and accessible models. Technology serves not just as a delivery mechanism, but as an enabler for deeper cognitive engagement.

Specifically, in the context of music education , digital tools have democratized access to composition, theory, and performance analysis. While technology cannot entirely replace the nuanced feedback of an in-person mentor, it allows students to experiment freely and learn at their own pace. Tools like digital audio workstations (DAWs) and interactive theory apps are fundamentally changing how musical concepts are grasped.

Where’s the Balance?

Popular Music and Traditional Music Education

Popular Music and Traditional Music Education

I think music education should find a balance between students’ own musical cultures and traditional musical skills rather than choosing one over the other. Students are more likely to engage when they can learn through music they already listen to and understand. At the same time, traditional skills such as musical literacy, harmony, rhythm, notation, and performance can give students a stronger foundation for creating and understanding music.

One idea from this module that I found particularly interesting was James Humberstone’s discussion of the traditional Western art music education pathway. His discussion made me think about how many music teachers come from successful formal music education backgrounds, while their experiences may not represent those of the majority of school students. This suggests that teachers should not assume that every student will connect with music in the same way.

Another important idea comes from Lucy Green’s research on informal music learning . Her work highlights ways in which popular musicians can learn through listening, copying recordings, working with friends, experimenting, and making music themselves. These approaches can give students more ownership over their musical learning.

Therefore, I would not remove traditional music education, but I would make it more flexible. Students could begin with music that is meaningful to them and then use it as a pathway to learn broader musical concepts and skills. In my view, the goal should be to connect traditional knowledge with contemporary musical cultures rather than treating them as opposing approaches.

My Key Takeaway

Effective music education does not have to choose between popular music and traditional music. Instead, teachers can use students’ existing musical interests as a starting point while gradually developing traditional musical knowledge, literacy, creativity, and performance skills.
