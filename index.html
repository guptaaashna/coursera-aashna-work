Where's the Balance?

Popular Music and Traditional Music Education

Where’s the Balance? Popular Music and Traditional Music Education

A reflection on balancing students’ musical cultures with traditional music skills, literacy, and Western art music in 21st-century education.

I believe music education needs a balance between traditional musical skills and the music students already experience in their everyday lives. Traditional approaches provide important foundations such as musical literacy, harmony, rhythm, listening, and performance skills.

At the same time, students should have opportunities to explore popular music and their own musical cultures. When learners are allowed to work with music that is meaningful to them, they can become more engaged and take greater ownership of their learning.

James Humberstone's discussions about Western art music education made me think about the importance of traditional musical knowledge. However, Lucy Green's work on informal music learning also shows the value of allowing students to learn through music that they already know and enjoy.

Therefore, I do not think traditional music education should disappear. Instead, it should become more flexible. Students can learn theory and traditional skills while also composing, performing, producing, and exploring popular music. The goal should be to create a music education that respects both musical heritage and students' contemporary musical identities.

My Provocation

Technology Should Serve Music Education — Not Replace It

Technology Should Serve Music Education — Not Replace It

A personal provocation about using digital technology thoughtfully in music education.

Technology has changed how music can be created, performed, recorded, shared, and learned. Digital audio workstations, notation software, online tutorials, recording tools, and interactive platforms can give learners opportunities that may not have been possible in a traditional classroom.

However, I question the assumption that adding more technology automatically makes music education better. Technology is only valuable when it improves the learning experience. The important question should be: What musical learning does this technology make possible or easier?

Research on technology-enhanced music learning has made me think more critically about this issue. Technology can support performance, composition, collaboration, experimentation, and feedback, but teachers still need to consider pedagogy, musical expression, creativity, and meaningful interaction.

My own view has therefore changed from seeing technology as automatically positive to seeing it as a tool that needs a clear educational purpose. A DAW should not be used simply because it is modern. It should be used when it helps students create, understand, communicate, or reflect on music in a meaningful way.

My provocation is simple: Music education should not ask how much technology we can add. It should ask what technology can help students learn that matters musically.

My Thoughts on Open Learning and Music Education

Reflection on Module Content

Share Your Thoughts Openly

My reflections on technology, open learning, music education, and Project Based Learning.

1. DAWs, sequencers and notation software

I would like to explore DAWs and step sequencers further because they provide creative ways to compose, arrange, and experiment with music. These technologies can also make music creation more accessible to learners who may not have extensive traditional instrumental training.

2. DJ-producers and sophisticated musical skills

Yes, I have been persuaded that DJ-producers have many sophisticated musical skills. Creating electronic music involves rhythm, harmony, structure, listening, sound selection, arrangement, mixing, and technological knowledge. The course helped me understand that using technology does not make the music-making process simple.

3. Has learning gone "OPEN"?

I agree with David Price that learning has increasingly gone "OPEN". Online platforms allow people to access courses, tutorials, resources, and learning communities beyond traditional classrooms. However, I think open learning works best when learners have the motivation and guidance to use these resources effectively.

4. Examples of OPEN learning

One example I found particularly useful was OpenLearn's Teaching Secondary Music course. It is a free course exploring musical learning, different teaching approaches, and formal, non-formal, and informal learning.

Explore OpenLearn Course →

I also found platforms such as Khan Academy useful because they demonstrate how digital platforms can make educational resources widely accessible.

5. Project Based Learning and Music Education

Project Based Learning can make music education more practical, creative, and collaborative. Students can compose, perform, produce, or record a project while developing problem-solving and teamwork skills. At the same time, music education can contribute creativity, communication, self-expression, and critical listening to Project Based Learning and education more broadly.

Final Reflection

Overall, this module made me see technology and open learning not simply as replacements for traditional education, but as tools that can expand how, where, and with whom music can be learned.
