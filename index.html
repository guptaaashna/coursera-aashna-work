Reflections on Technology and Education

The intersection of technology and education is rapidly evolving. We are moving away from traditional rote learning towards more interactive, personalized, and accessible models. Technology can serve not just as a delivery mechanism, but also as an enabler for deeper learning.

Specifically, in the context of music education, digital tools have expanded opportunities for composition, theory, experimentation, and performance analysis. Technology cannot entirely replace the nuanced feedback of an in-person mentor, but it can allow students to experiment freely and learn at their own pace. Tools such as digital audio workstations and interactive learning platforms are changing how musical concepts can be explored.

Where's the Balance?

Popular Music and Traditional Music Education

Where's the Balance? Popular Music and Traditional Music Education

Music education in the 21st century should find a balance between traditional musical knowledge and the musical cultures that students already engage with. Students today listen to and create many different forms of popular music, so their existing musical interests should have a meaningful place in the classroom.

Traditional music education still provides important foundations. Skills such as reading music, understanding harmony and rhythm, developing technique, and studying Western art music can give students a strong musical vocabulary. James Humberstone's discussion of the Western art music educational pathway made me think about how strongly traditional approaches have shaped music education.

At the same time, Lucy Green's research on informal learning demonstrates the value of allowing students to learn through popular music, peer collaboration, listening, copying, improvisation, and their own musical choices. This approach can make students feel more connected to the learning process.

I therefore believe that the choice should not be between popular music and traditional music. Instead, teachers can combine both. Students can learn musical theory through music they already enjoy while also being introduced to unfamiliar styles, composers, instruments, and traditions.

The goal should be to create a flexible music education where traditional skills remain valuable but are not separated from students' contemporary musical lives. This balance can help students become technically capable musicians while also encouraging creativity, curiosity, and personal expression.

Technology Should Serve Music Education — Not Replace It

My Provocation

My provocation is simple: technology should serve music education, not become the purpose of music education.

Technology has changed the way people create, perform, record, share, and learn music. Digital audio workstations, notation software, online tutorials, virtual instruments, recording tools, and interactive learning platforms have made musical experimentation more accessible than ever. These tools can give students opportunities to compose, collaborate, receive feedback, and explore sounds that may not otherwise be available to them.

However, I question the assumption that adding more technology automatically creates better music education. A classroom can contain many digital tools and still provide a weak learning experience if those tools are not connected to meaningful musical goals. Technology can become distracting when students focus more on operating software than on listening, creating, performing, understanding, and expressing music.

A systematic review by Mohammadian, Pourroostaei Ardakani, and Zavaraki (2026) examined 30 studies of technology-enhanced music learning. The review found that pedagogical challenges were more significant than technological barriers in the studies examined. It also highlighted a need to balance technical proficiency with areas such as aesthetic development and musical expression.

This research challenged one of my own assumptions. Before exploring the topic, I tended to think of technology as naturally positive in education. I now think the more important question is not "How much technology can we use?" but "What musical learning does this technology make possible?"

For example, a digital audio workstation can allow a student to experiment with composition and sound design. Recording software can help students listen critically to their own performances. Online collaboration can connect learners who are physically far apart. These are meaningful uses because the technology expands what students can do.

At the same time, some parts of music education should remain deeply human. Listening together, performing with others, receiving guidance from a teacher, discussing musical choices, and experiencing the emotional side of music cannot simply be reduced to software features.

My provocation, therefore, is that music educators should be selective rather than automatically enthusiastic about technology. A digital tool should be introduced because it supports creativity, accessibility, collaboration, musical understanding, or expression — not simply because it is new.

The future of music education should not be technology versus traditional teaching. Instead, technology should become one part of a broader learning environment that combines musical knowledge, creativity, human interaction, experimentation, and personal expression.
